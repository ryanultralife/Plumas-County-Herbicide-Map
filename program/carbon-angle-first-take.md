# The carbon angle: a first take

**Bottom line.** There is a real argument here, but it is not the one in the original framing. The version that survives scrutiny is narrow and procedural, and since the first draft it has been verified in CARB's own spreadsheet: the grant calculator that scores CAL FIRE reforestation projects charges the carbon in killed brush only against acres entered as "site preparation," while acres entered as "herbicide treatment" carry a token diesel factor and nothing else, and no formula links the two fields. California's compliance offset protocol, separately, excludes shrub, litter, duff and (absent mechanical disturbance) soil carbon from its accounting boundary by design. The version that does not survive is the physical one. The best long-term experiments in this exact region find that vegetation control does not measurably reduce soil carbon, that the standing-biomass deficit closes by about age 20, and that killed foliage is a carbon input to the forest floor rather than a loss from it. If we publish, we publish an accounting-transparency piece with a documented funding chain, not a "spraying releases carbon" piece. A single net-tonnage or dollar figure would not hold up.

## What the accounting actually counts

CARB's Compliance Offset Protocol for U.S. Forest Projects (adopted June 25, 2015, still the operative version) excludes shrub and herbaceous understory carbon entirely from Improved Forest Management and Avoided Conversion projects, with no baseline and no project value (Tables 4.2 and 4.3). Lying dead wood and litter/duff are excluded outright in all project types. Soil carbon enters the boundary only if site preparation involves deep ripping, furrowing, or plowing over more than 25 percent of the area, or if mechanical site prep is off-contour, and "No crediting of increased soil carbon is allowed" (Tables 4.1 to 4.3). Broadcast herbicide disturbs no soil surface and triggers neither condition. That is the checkable core, and it holds.

One thing we must not say. For Reforestation projects, understory carbon (RF-2) is included in the baseline and zeroed in the project scenario, with a note that brush removal is "quantified in secondary emissions" that leads to a chapter containing only diesel combustion and cropland-shifting leakage (subchapter 5.1.5, Equations 5.2 to 5.4). That is a genuine dangling cross-reference, but it is a drafting error, not a loophole. Because crediting is baseline minus project, keeping the brush in the baseline makes it a permanent subtraction from creditable tons. Publishing it as an escape hatch would earn us a correction.

The bigger correction is one of regime. The offset protocol governs cap-and-trade credits. The Humbug project is a CAL FIRE Forest Health grant, which is Greenhouse Gas Reduction Fund spending, and its carbon is estimated with CARB and CAL FIRE's separate Forest Restoration and Management Quantification Methodology. That methodology explicitly subtracts understory carbon at 3.6, 13.9 and 24.0 MT CO2e per acre by cover class, and charges herbicide treatment at 0.0607 MT CO2e per acre (FRM QM April 26, 2021, Equations 1 to 3; November 5, 2025 draft technical documentation, Tables 1 and 2). So "this carbon is invisible to California's climate accounting" is false as stated for the grant program.

The residual gap is narrower, and it is now a finding rather than an inference. We opened the Benefits Calculator itself (FRM_Calculator_Tool20-21.xlsx, dated April 26, 2021, "Corrected February 8, 2022," the tool paired with the 2021 methodology; and the FY25-26 draft dated November 5, 2025). On the Reforestation sheet a grantee enters "Area subject to site preparation (acres)" in C25 and "Area subject to herbicide treatment (acres)" in C26. The formulas, verbatim from the workbook: the project scenario is `F18 = C23*3.67 - (mobile-combustion factor by brush cover)*C25 - C26*0.0607`, and the baseline is `F19 = C21*3.67 + (3.6 | 13.9 | 24.0 by land cover)*C25`. The understory carbon term is multiplied by C25 only. C26 appears in exactly one place in the entire workbook, the 0.0607 diesel term, and there is no formula, validation rule, or comment that links C26 to C25 or to the reforestation acreage. The draft FY25-26 tool restructures the sheet (cells E23 and E24) but keeps the same logic. Worked through with the workbook's own factors and heavy-shrub selections: 100 acres entered as herbicide-only costs the project 6.07 t CO2e; the same 100 acres entered as site preparation costs 2,442.9 t. The methodology's own definitions (draft technical documentation, p. 50) keep the two activities separate: site preparation is "the removal or alterations of fuels or ground material to prepare for reforestation. Includes constructing piles, soil ripping, etc." and herbicide treatment is "application of herbicide to target and kill undesirable plants prior to planting." So a project that kills its brush chemically and reports it that way books the brush carbon at roughly 0.1 percent of what the same brush would cost if it were removed with a machine. Whether any Plumas grantee has actually filed that way is the next records request, not something we can assert.

One oddity in the posted FY25-26 draft is worth passing to CARB rather than publishing: its baseline formula (Reforestation!H15) multiplies E15, the "Year of planting" cell, by 3.67 as if it were the baseline carbon stock, and nothing in the workbook references E19, the cell labelled as that stock. In the 2021 tool the analogous formula correctly uses the carbon cell. That reads as a cell-reference error in a draft, and it is CARB's to fix.

## What the science supports

Established. Powers, Busse, McFarlane, Zhang and Young (Forestry 86(1):47-58, 2013) pooled 21 paired soil observations across 18 long-term western sites, 10 to 35 years post-treatment, and found no significant effect of understory control on carbon in the top 30 cm of mineral soil. Their conclusion is depth-qualified ("beyond the upper 10 cm") and stage-qualified ("once trees dominate the site"), and forest floor mass was 15 percent greater where understory was retained (P = 0.03). The same paper documents that more than twice as much carbon sits in vegetation when the understory is counted, while control raised overstory biomass by 22.1 Mg/ha. Zhang et al. (2017, LTSP installations in the Sierra Nevada and Southern Cascades) found total aboveground biomass significantly lower under vegetation control at ages 5 and 10 but statistically indistinguishable at age 20.

McFarlane, Schoenholtz and Powers (SSSAJ 73:1020-1032, 2009) found herbicide had no effect on total belowground carbon at three northern California plantations and increased forest-floor carbon 35 percent at the poorest site. Their mineral-soil null is underpowered (minimum detectable difference greater than 24 Mg C/ha at two sites, greater than 40 at Feather Falls), so it is not evidence of no effect.

Contested. Zhang, Zhang, Mattson and Finley (Forests 11:997, 2020) at Challenge Experimental Forest, Yuba County on the Plumas National Forest, report aboveground vegetation carbon of 85 ± 14 Mg C/ha with no vegetation control against 61 ± 6 with control at age 28, and 114 ± 11 against 106 ± 11 at age 54. Total ecosystem carbon was 19 to 23 percent higher without control at age 28 (p < 0.05) but not significantly different at age 54 (p > 0.36). Soil carbon at age 54 was significantly greater without control (p < 0.03), by roughly 8 Mg C/ha averaged across spacings; the frequently quoted +18.4 figure is a single spacing cell and the interaction test was not significant (p > 0.11). Two blocks, one site, and the treatment was 2,4,5-T plus five years of hand weeding, not glyphosate or imazapyr. The authors conclude in favor of early vegetation control. We must say so whenever we cite them.

Nitrogen. This is the most robust part of the mechanism. Busse (PSW-GTR-178, 2000) measured Ceanothus velutinus fixing an average of 10 kg N/ha/yr (maximum 15) under interior pine canopies, against dry deposition of about 1 kg N/ha/yr, so shrub fixation supplies roughly half of annual nitrogen input; about 6 kg N/ha/yr replaces what an overstory harvest removes. At Feather Falls, where N-fixing Ceanothus integerrimus dominates, herbicide lowered forest-floor N by 1.2 g N/kg and raised C/N by 16 percent, and cut fine-root N from 8.3 to 7.0 g/kg (McFarlane et al. 2009). No published fixation rate exists for C. integerrimus or C. cordulatus specifically. Do not extrapolate the 20 to 100 kg/ha/yr Oregon shrub-field numbers to Plumas.

## The money

CAL FIRE's Forest Health Program is a California Climate Investments program drawing GGRF appropriations from Cap-and-Invest auction proceeds. CARB's funded-programs table (as of October 2025) shows CAL FIRE's Sustainable Forests line at $1,459M prior to FY25-26 plus $200M in FY25-26, for $1,659M cumulative. Caveat that belongs in the same sentence: GGRF is not the program's only source. LAO's April 2026 handout lists the fund source as "Various," $990M multiyear across 2020-21 through 2025-26, excluding Proposition 4 bond money, and CAL FIRE now runs parallel CCI and Prop 4 solicitations. Tying a specific award to auction dollars requires that award's own documentation.

The Humbug filing is real and quotable. CEQA SCH 2024041007 (lead agency Feather River RCD; project applicant Lassen Forest IV Humbug, LLC and RRF Lassen-Plumas LLC; Plumas County, 2,196 acres, Notice of Exemption received 4/24/2024) states that "CAL FIRE Forest Health grant funding, in addition to landowner contributions, will support removal of unmerchantable burned trees from the project area, control of competing vegetation with herbicide and planting of conifer seedlings," with a stated benefit to "increase the storage of CO2." It was filed exempt under the declared-emergency provision, PRC 21080(b)(3) and Guidelines 15269(a). The filing does not cite any executive order number; earlier drafts of ours attributed it to EO N-13-21, which appears nowhere in the document. Seven of eight post-Dixie filings naming herbicide were exemptions, so the herbicide received no CEQA analysis and no comment period. Only the Upper Butte Creek project (SCH 2026070148) got a Negative Declaration.

Disclosure exists but is incidental. Our first pass found the Collins Almanor project in CARB's 2026 CCI All Implemented Projects dataset under an identifier of 8GG21605, with $4,965,493 implemented, 2,619,493 MTCO2e of claimed reductions, and a description that says the money pays for "spraying these units (herbicide application)." The dollar figure matches the award list exactly; the 8GG identifier does not appear in any CAL FIRE award list and could not be located by search, so re-read it from the CCI dataset before citing it. The structural point stands on its own: only 18 of 172,184 project rows statewide contain the word herbicide, there is no herbicide field, and no acreage or quantity of active ingredient is reported anywhere. That is the publishable point: not that spraying is hidden, but that it is unquantified.

## The math, roughly

Start with the denominator, because it is where we would most easily discredit ourselves. Plumas 2024 PUR reports 66,783 herbicide acre-treatments. Collapsing records that share date, owner, location and acreage (one spray event filed once per product) gives 38,679, a factor of 1.73. Collapsing repeat visits further is only partly defensible: a PLSS section is 640 acres, and in 2024 only 19 of 112 sections exceed that ceiling, forcing 7,052 acres of repeat visits and yielding about 31,627 unique acres. The more aggressive 22,545 figure assumes every record one owner filed inside a square mile is the same block, which the acreage distribution contradicts. Report unique treated ground as roughly 22,500 to 38,700 acres, central near 31,600, and never as a single "3x correction."

On the numerator, the defensible statement is gross, not net. Standing shrub plus root carbon put at risk by a broadcast treatment runs roughly 1.8 to 44.6 t CO2e per acre with a central estimate near 11.5, built from 2 to 20 Mg/ha aboveground shrub biomass (Ritchie, Zhang and Hammett 2019 measured about 12.6 Mg/ha of shrub in a 12-year-old northeastern California plantation) and a root:shoot ratio near 0.6 (Kummerow, Krause and Jow 1977, five-species California chaparral). Three caveats belong in the same breath as that number. It is gross: Zhang et al. 2017 found the released conifers add about 52 Mg/ha of tree biomass, roughly 36 t CO2e per acre, closing the gap by age 20. It is carbon at risk, not carbon emitted: herbicide makes necromass that decays over years, and resprouters keep their root crowns. And it must not be multiplied by the August 2026 allowance price of $32.48 per tonne, because that price values a tonne emitted now. We should publish no dollar figure.

## Where this argument is weak

Weakest link first: the stated mechanism is partly backwards. Dead foliage is an input to the forest floor. McFarlane measured forest-floor mass rising 45 percent under herbicide at the poorest site. The real costs are foregone productivity and degraded litter and nitrogen quality, not the fate of the necromass.

Second, the soil-carbon claim has one good multi-site test and it comes out against us. Third, Ritchie et al. 2019 found chemically released plots carried more total aboveground biomass (17.1 Mg/ha) than unsprayed controls (13.3). Fourth, reforestation failure is documented locally: eleven years after the 2007 Moonlight Fire, 53.9 percent of public land converted to shrubland against 2.2 percent of private land that was 99.6 percent herbicide-treated (Stephens et al., FEM 468:118161, 2020). Fifth, our two most-cited papers, Zhang 2020 and Powers 2013, both conclude in favor of the practice. Sixth, the CCI methodology does have an understory line item, so the blanket "invisible" claim is refutable from a public PDF. The precise claim, that the line item is keyed to site-preparation acres and not to herbicide acres, is the one to make, and it is the one we have now checked cell by cell.

The counter-case has its own soft spot, which we can note without leaning on: in the Storrie/Chips reburn analysis, shrub cover mattered only under cooler conditions, while under hot conditions severity tracked snag basal area (Coppoletta, Merriam and Collins, Ecological Applications 26:686-699, 2016).
